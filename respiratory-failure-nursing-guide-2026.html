Type 1 vs Type 2 Respiratory Failure

FeatureType 1 (Hypoxemic)Type 2 (Hypercapnic)
DefinitionLow PaO2 (<60 mmHg) with normal or low PaCO2; problem is OXYGENATIONElevated PaCO2 (>50 mmHg) with respiratory acidosis; problem is VENTILATION (can't eliminate CO2)
MechanismV/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion impairment, low FiO2Reduced respiratory drive, neuromuscular weakness, airway obstruction, chest wall restriction — insufficient alveolar ventilation
Common CausesARDS, pneumonia, pulmonary edema, PE, pneumothorax, atelectasisCOPD exacerbation, opioid overdose, neuromuscular disease (Guillain-Barré, myasthenia gravis), severe asthma, obesity hypoventilation
ABG FindingspH normal or high; PaO2 low (<60); PaCO2 normal or low; SaO2 lowpH low (<7.35 if acute); PaCO2 high (>50); HCO3 normal (acute) or high (compensated chronic); PaO2 may be low

Signs of impending respiratory failure requiring immediate escalation: RR >35, SpO2 <90% on high-flow O2, altered mental status (CO2 narcosis or hypoxemia), inability to speak full sentences, paradoxical breathing (abdomen moves in while chest moves out).

COPD and oxygen: The "hypoxic drive" concern is real but often overstated. Hypoxic COPD patients should STILL receive O2 for SpO2 <88–92%; target SpO2 88–92% (not 95–100%). Use Venturi mask for precise delivery. Do NOT withhold O2 from a COPD patient in distress.

Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)

ModeSettingsIndicationsContraindications
CPAP (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure)Single constant pressure (5–20 cmH2O); patient breathes spontaneously; maintains PEEP onlyObstructive sleep apnea; cardiogenic pulmonary edema; Type 1 respiratory failure; post-extubation supportRespiratory arrest; inability to protect airway; hemodynamic instability; unable to tolerate mask
BiPAP (Bilevel Positive Airway Pressure)IPAP (inspiratory pressure 10–20 cmH2O) + EPAP (expiratory pressure 4–8 cmH2O); helps both ventilation AND oxygenationCOPD exacerbation (first-line, reduces intubation 50%+); CHF; neuromuscular disease; OHS; Type 2 respiratory failureSame as CPAP contraindications; vomiting or high aspiration risk; recent facial surgery; inability to fit mask

NIV Nursing Care

  • Fit mask carefully (tight enough to seal, loose enough to prevent pressure injury)
  • Apply barrier dressing to nose bridge to prevent pressure ulcer
  • Patient must have adequate mental status to remove mask if needed
  • Continuous SpO2 and RR monitoring; assess every hour for tolerance and response
  • Offer breaks every 2–4 hours for skin assessment, oral care, medications
  • Have intubation equipment at bedside — NIV can fail rapidly
  • Document patient's response to NIV after 1–2 hours; persistent distress = escalate to intubation

ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome)

Berlin Definition (2012)

  • Acute onset within 1 week of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms
  • Bilateral opacities on chest imaging (not explained by effusions, atelectasis, or nodules)
  • NOT fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload (TRALI distinction)
  • P/F ratio (PaO2/FiO2): Mild = 200–300; Moderate = 100–200; Severe = <100

Common ARDS triggers: sepsis (most common, 40%), pneumonia, aspiration, trauma, massive transfusion, pancreatitis, drowning, inhalation injury.

Nursing Priorities in ARDS

  • Lung-protective ventilation: 6 mL/kg IBW tidal volume; Pplat ≤30 cmH2O
  • PEEP titration per ARDSnet table to maintain oxygenation
  • Prone positioning: 16+ hr/day for P/F <150 reduces mortality significantly; requires dedicated team; monitor skin, eye, tube security
  • Fluid management: conservative strategy once hemodynamically stable (negative fluid balance improves lung function)
  • Avoid oxygen toxicity: wean FiO2 below 0.60 as quickly as tolerated
  • Sedation and analgesia per ABCDEF bundle
  • Daily spontaneous awakening + breathing trials

COPD Exacerbation

Acute worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal day-to-day variation. Most common trigger: respiratory infection (viral URI 50%).

COPD Exacerbation Management

  • Oxygen: Target SpO2 88–92% (not higher); Venturi mask preferred for precise delivery
  • Bronchodilators: SABA (albuterol) + SAMA (ipratropium) nebulizers/MDI; scheduled and PRN
  • Systemic corticosteroids: Methylprednisolone or prednisone 40 mg x 5 days (reduces treatment failure and length of stay)
  • Antibiotics: If purulent sputum, increased sputum volume, or increased dyspnea; amoxicillin-clavulanate, azithromycin, doxycycline
  • Non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP): First-line for moderate-severe exacerbation with respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35); reduces intubation rate by 50%
  • Intubation: If BiPAP fails or patient cannot protect airway

NCLEX High-Yield Points

  • Type 1 = oxygenation failure (low PaO2, normal/low CO2); Type 2 = ventilation failure (high CO2)
  • CO poisoning: SpO2 may be falsely NORMAL (oximetry can't distinguish COHb from OxyHgb); use co-oximetry; treat with 100% O2
  • COPD target SpO2: 88–92% (not 95–100%); Venturi mask for controlled delivery
  • Non-rebreather mask: highest non-invasive FiO2 delivery; reservoir bag must stay inflated
  • BiPAP for COPD exacerbation: reduces intubation need; IPAP helps inspiration, EPAP = PEEP
  • ARDS: bilateral infiltrates + P/F ratio <300; Berlin criteria; lung-protective ventilation + prone positioning
  • Prone positioning: for ARDS P/F <150; 16+ hours/day; significant mortality reduction
  • Signs of impending respiratory arrest: RR >35, SpO2 <90% on high-flow, altered consciousness, paradoxical breathing
