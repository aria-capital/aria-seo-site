Adenosine: The ICU Nurse's Guide

⚕️ Medical Disclaimer: This content is for educational purposes only and is intended for licensed healthcare professionals. It does not constitute medical advice and should not replace clinical judgment, facility protocols, or physician orders. Always verify medications, doses, and procedures with your institution's guidelines.

Adenosine is the drug that stops the heart to fix it. Given as a lightning-fast push, it interrupts a re-entrant tachycardia by briefly blocking the AV node — the monitor flatlines for a few seconds, then a normal rhythm usually resumes. It's dramatic, safe when used correctly, and almost entirely dependent on nursing technique to work.

The short version: Adenosine is first-line for stable, regular, narrow-complex SVT (specifically AV-nodal re-entrant tachycardias). Dose it 6 mg rapid IV push, then 12 mg if needed, each followed by an immediate rapid saline flush. Its half-life is seconds, so speed is everything. Expect a brief period of asystole/pause and warn the patient. Avoid in irregular or wide-complex tachycardia of uncertain origin and in reactive airway disease.

How adenosine works

Adenosine activates receptors in the AV node, transiently blocking conduction. In AV-nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT) and AV re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT), the re-entry circuit depends on the AV node, so a momentary block breaks the loop and lets the sinus node take back over. Its extraordinarily short half-life — on the order of ten seconds — is why the effect is so brief and why technique is non-negotiable: give it slowly and it's metabolized before it reaches the heart.

The dose and the technique

The classic sequence is 6 mg rapid IV push, and if the rhythm doesn't convert within a couple of minutes, 12 mg (a second 12 mg dose may follow per protocol). But the number is only half the story — how you give it determines whether it works.

Bedside rule of thumb: "Fast push, fast flush, closest port." A large proximal IV, a two-syringe technique, and an instant flush are what make adenosine work. Given too slowly, it simply won't convert the rhythm.

What to expect — and warn the patient about

After the push, the monitor typically shows a few seconds of pause or asystole before the rhythm resumes — this is expected, not a code. Patients often feel a brief, intense sense of chest pressure, flushing, or impending doom, so a quick warning ("you may feel a strange, uncomfortable sensation for a few seconds; it passes quickly") is part of good care. Transient facial flushing, dyspnea, and chest discomfort are common and short-lived.

Diagnostic use

Even when adenosine doesn't convert a rhythm, the transient AV block can unmask the underlying atrial activity — for instance revealing the flutter waves of atrial flutter — which helps clarify a fast, regular rhythm you couldn't quite read.

Where adenosine is the wrong choice

Know the contraindications. Adenosine is for regular, narrow-complex SVT. Avoid it in irregular wide-complex tachycardia (possible AF with pre-excitation/WPW), where AV-nodal blockade can accelerate conduction down an accessory pathway and precipitate ventricular fibrillation. Use caution or avoid in severe asthma/reactive airway disease (it can cause bronchospasm), in high-grade AV block or sick sinus without a pacemaker, and after cardiac transplant (denervated hearts are hypersensitive — much lower doses).

Interactions

Theophylline and caffeine are adenosine antagonists and blunt its effect — a patient may need a higher dose or the drug may fail. Dipyridamole potentiates adenosine and can dangerously prolong its effect, so doses are reduced. Carbamazepine may increase the degree of heart block. Always check what the patient is already on.

Related pharmacology: for rate control of atrial fibrillation with RVR (not adenosine's job), see diltiazem; for the broad-spectrum antiarrhythmic, see amiodarone; and for torsades, magnesium.

Bottom line

Adenosine is a fast, elegant fix for stable, regular, narrow-complex SVT — but only if you give it right. The dose is 6 then 12 mg, and the technique (closest port, instant push, immediate flush) is the drug. Expect a brief pause on the monitor, warn the patient about the strange sensation, and steer clear of it in irregular wide-complex rhythms and severe asthma. Used correctly, it's one of the most satisfying interventions in critical care: a few seconds of stillness, then a normal heartbeat.
